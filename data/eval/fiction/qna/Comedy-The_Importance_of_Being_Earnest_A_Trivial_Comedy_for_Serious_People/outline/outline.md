# The Importance of Being Earnest - Outline

Oscar Wilde's comedy (1895) follows two young gentlemen who lead double lives. Jack Worthing is a respectable landowner and the guardian of his young ward, Cecily Cardew, at his country house. To escape his duties he has invented a wild younger brother called Ernest who lives in London, and in town he goes by the name Ernest himself. His friend Algernon Moncrieff uses a similar trick: he has invented an invalid friend, Bunbury, whose sudden illnesses let him leave town whenever he wants to avoid a social duty. Algernon calls this habit "Bunburying".

Jack loves Gwendolen Fairfax, Algernon's cousin, who says she can only love a man named Ernest. Her mother, Lady Bracknell, refuses the match when she learns that Jack was found as a baby in a handbag at Victoria Station and does not know who his parents are. Algernon then travels to Jack's country house pretending to be the wicked brother Ernest and falls in love with Cecily, who has long imagined being engaged to Ernest. When Gwendolen arrives, both women believe they are engaged to the same Ernest, and the men's lies come out. Both men arrange to be christened Ernest by the local rector, Canon Chasuble.

The truth is revealed through Cecily's governess, Miss Prism. Twenty-eight years earlier she mistakenly left a baby in a handbag at the station and put the manuscript of her novel in the pram instead. That baby was Jack, the son of Lady Bracknell's sister and therefore Algernon's older brother, and he had been christened Ernest. The play ends with three engagements: Jack and Gwendolen, Algernon and Cecily, and Canon Chasuble and Miss Prism. Jack closes with the remark that he now understands the vital importance of being earnest.
